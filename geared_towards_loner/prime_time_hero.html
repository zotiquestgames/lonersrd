Prime Time Hero

Introduction

Prime Time Hero is a solo tabletop RPG adventure set in the 1980s of network television, where every problem can be solved before the last commercial break, and somebody has to solve it.

This minimalist Solo Role Playing Game is designed to play like the action-adventure shows of the decade: MacGyver, Knight Rider, Magnum P.I., The A-Team. You are the hero of your own series. Somebody needs help, the people who should give it won’t, and you have a car, a skill, and a code you don’t break.

Every episode is a new case. One week it’s a developer leaning on a fishing village in Hawaii; the next it’s a forged Monet at a Monte Carlo auction. You get through it on wits and charm, and with your fists when those run out.

Prime Time Hero follows the following design principles:

  1. Portable: to play you will need a few common (six-sided) dice and writing materials. Anything else is optional and not essential.
  2. Rules-Light: the game relies on a few rules and only one solving mechanic, easy to learn and eventually to memorize.
  3. Tag-based: characters and situations are defined only by qualitative descriptors and no quantitative characteristics.
  4. Emergent & Open-Ended: there are no pre-written stories; every session generates its own narrative through choices and oracle answers.

With a focus on quick resolutions, Prime Time Hero throws you headfirst into the heart of the action. Your character will be defined by thematic tags, like “Reformed Con Artist”, “Retired Cat Burglar”, or “Jury-Rigging Genius”.

What is a Role Playing Game (RPG)?

In a role-playing game, you play a character. The choices you make determine what happens to them and to the world around them. You are not reading a story someone else wrote; you are building it as you play. Your character has a goal, abilities, and a world to move through. What happens next is not predetermined.

Traditional role-playing games are played with a group and a Game Master who prepares the world and referees what happens. Prime Time Hero removes both requirements. You play alone. The Oracle, a simple dice mechanic, replaces the Game Master’s unpredictability. You are player and storyteller both.

What is a Solo RPG?

In a solo RPG a single player takes on the role of one or more characters while also managing some elements of the game world. Unlike a gamebook (such as the Fighting Fantasy, Lone Wolf, and Tunnels & Trolls series) a solo RPG is not a form of interactive, forked narrative in which outcomes are pre-determined and limited by the author’s choices.

Through the interaction of player, oracle, tools, and prompts, the character’s actions will build an emergent narrative within whose boundaries anything can be attempted, without predetermined limits.

Playing Safely

You control what happens. Prime Time Hero lets you explore any story, but you also set the limits. One rule covers it: if a scene makes you uncomfortable, change it. Rewind, reframe, or stop entirely. The game is a tool for telling stories you want to tell; nothing here requires you to push through something that doesn’t feel right.

Minimum Requirements

To play Prime Time Hero you will need:

  • 4 six-sided dice (also known as d6s): two pairs of different colors
  • Paper and writing tools: at least a sheet of scrap paper and a pencil, but index cards or sticky notes are a fine addition
  • Character sheet: you may use the provided sheet at the back or a simple index card.

Optional but useful:

  • Notebook: Prime Time Hero is not a solo journaling game, you can easily play it in the “theater of mind”. But keeping track of characters, locations, and ongoing storylines helps keep things consistent across sessions.

The 1980s Action-Adventure World

The world of Prime Time Hero is a heightened version of the 1980s, where:

  • Technology is cutting-edge but analog: Computers have green screens, phones are car-mounted, and every gadget has blinking lights
  • Heroes have codes of honor: Violence is a last resort and innocent people come first. The good guys are supposed to win; the Oracle decides what it costs them
  • Locations are exotic but accessible: From Hawaiian beaches to Swiss ski lodges, adventure is always one flight away
  • Problems are personal: Every case involves helping regular people against corrupt officials, greedy corporations, or international criminals
  • Style matters: Fast cars, designer suits, and perfectly feathered hair are essential tools of the trade
  • Everything is slightly over-the-top: Explosions are bigger, stunts are crazier, and one-liners are snappier

Playable Themes

  • Heists, cons, and corporate espionage. Boardrooms hide more crimes than back alleys, and sometimes the only way to beat a thief is to out-plan them.
  • International intrigue. Embassies, borders, and Cold War leftovers: every exotic location has someone watching it.
  • Small-town corruption. One crooked sheriff or greedy developer, and a town that needs someone from outside to stand up for it.
  • The little guy against the machine. Polluters, slumlords, and frauds who assume nobody will fight back.
  • Personal vendettas. Old partners, old enemies, family secrets; the past always finds the hero’s address.
  • Fame and its price. Studios, stadiums, and celebrity scandals, where the cameras are part of the danger.

Timeline

The series runs through a heightened 1980s. These are the headlines the hero’s world grew out of; each episode takes place wherever in the decade you set it.

  • 1980: The Iran hostage crisis fills every newscast. The public starts wanting heroes who get things done without waiting on Washington.
  • 1981: MTV goes on the air. Image becomes a language, and everyone from senators to smugglers learns to speak it.
  • 1982: The Falklands War puts a French anti-ship missile on every front page. Arms dealers get the best advertising of the decade for free.
  • 1983: The Strategic Defense Initiative is announced. Every defense contractor wants a slice of the “Star Wars” budget, and some will steal to get it.
  • 1984: Personal computers land on ordinary office desks, and ordinary offices learn they can be broken into over a phone line.
  • 1985: French agents sink the Greenpeace ship Rainbow Warrior in Auckland harbor. Governments, it turns out, will bomb activists too.
  • 1986: Chernobyl burns and the Iran-Contra affair breaks. Secrets are leaking from both sides of the Iron Curtain.
  • 1987: Black Monday wipes out fortunes in a single day. Some of the people who lost everything want someone to blame; some of the people who didn’t knew it was coming.
  • 1988: A bomb brings down a passenger jet over Lockerbie, Scotland. Airport security becomes everybody’s problem, and every customs line becomes a place where a cover can fail.
  • 1989: The Berlin Wall falls. A generation of spies is suddenly out of work, and their skills are for sale.

Characters

In the world of Prime Time Hero, you are the name in the opening credits. You have a past the pilot episode only hinted at, a ride everyone recognizes, and a line you won’t cross no matter who is paying.

Everything is a Character

In Prime Time Hero Non-Playing Characters (NPCs), Foes, Organizations, Monsters, and even relevant objects like vehicles are characters too!

Your Protagonist is described by some fixed traits:

  • Name: The name should be iconic and consistent with the tone and setting of the story.
  • Concept: A concise description of the character’s profession, background, and abilities. The best are adjective-noun pairings, like “Rogue CIA Agent” or “Hawaiian Private Eye”.
  • Skills (x2): Abilities not necessarily character-specific but not characteristics common to all. “Smart” is not a skill, “Jury-Rigging Genius” is.
  • Frailty: Something that could potentially get in the way of the character, either physically, mentally, or socially.
  • Gear (x2): Particular equipment supplied to the character in coherence with the setting. Everyday items are taken for granted and do not fall under this trait.
  • Goal: The long-term objective.
  • Motive: What drives the pursuit of the goal.
  • Nemesis: A person or organization that hinders the protagonist. Optional at character creation; it can emerge directly from play, and may or may not be the direct antagonist of the story.
  • Luck: The measure of a character’s ability to avoid ill fortune or an inauspicious outcome. It applies only in Conflicts and resets fully when each conflict begins and ends. Luck starts and caps at 6.

These traits are described by tags, descriptive words or phrases that can identify anything in the game world. Even the details of the environment in which the action moves and conditions (physical or mental) of the characters are tags.

They are qualitative representations. They are not quantitative measures.

When a tag is relevant to a situation it may grant Advantage or Disadvantage on an oracle roll. A tag can be real and present without bearing on the current situation; relevance is the test, not presence.

Relationships

When a connection with an NPC has been tested, strained, or deepened through play, that relationship earns a tag, such as Trusted Informant, Sworn Enemy, or Uneasy Ally. Relationship tags function like any other: when they bear directly on the situation, they grant Advantage or Disadvantage. They are earned in the fiction, not assigned at creation.

When to Promote a Tag to a Character

If an element matters once, keep it as plain tags. If it keeps showing up or pushes back against the Protagonist, give it a character sheet and play it by the same rules.

Rules

Prime Time Hero is a minimalist Solo Role Playing Game designed to be played with only one character (the Protagonist). You’ll guide them through the story that will unravel during the game, asking closed questions to an Oracle which will help you overturn your expectations.

Every now and then you will be surprised with an unexpected twist!

Framing a Scene

Before asking the Oracle anything, establish the scene’s conditions. Answer three questions:

  • Where is the Protagonist, and what does that place feel like right now?
  • Who is present, or likely to become present?
  • What is the Protagonist’s goal for this scene?

Use what the fiction has established. If something is genuinely uncertain, ask the Oracle one framing question before the scene begins. A framing question must be about a fictional condition (what is already true about the situation), not about what will happen. Once all three are answered, the scene is open.

Closing a Scene

A scene ends when one of the following is true:

  • The Protagonist’s immediate goal has been resolved: achieved or definitively failed.
  • The goal has been blocked: circumstances have made it impossible to pursue further here.
  • The Protagonist abandons the goal and commits to a different course of action.

When any of these conditions is met, stop asking Oracle questions. Note the outcome, then roll the Scene Transition. If none feel clearly met, ask: “Has this scene run its course?” A Yes of any kind closes it. A No means there is still something here worth pursuing.

Consulting the Oracle

When you need to test the narrative, you’ll ask a closed question (answerable with Yes or No) to the Oracle. Roll 1 Chance Die and 1 Risk Die of different colors, then follow these two steps.

  • Step 1: Compare the Dice
    • Chance Die higher → Yes
    • Risk Die higher → No
    • Both equal → Yes, but… (+1 Twist Counter). Stop here; Step 2 does not apply to equal rolls.
  • Step 2: Check for Modifiers (only when the dice are not equal)
    • Both dice ≤ 3 → add but…
    • Both dice ≥ 4 → add and…
Result Outcome
Chance > Risk Yes
Chance > Risk, both ≤ 3 Yes, but…
Chance > Risk, both ≥ 4 Yes, and…
Risk > Chance No
Risk > Chance, both ≤ 3 No, but…
Risk > Chance, both ≥ 4 No, and…
Both equal Yes, but… (+1 Twist Counter)

Yes… You get what you want.

Advantage and Disadvantage

Tags, conditions, and circumstances can grant Advantage or Disadvantage.

  • Advantage → Roll 2 Chance Dice, keep the highest.
  • Disadvantage → Roll 2 Risk Dice, keep the highest.
  • Read the result from the kept die only. A discarded die counts for nothing: equal dice and the but…/and… bands are checked after discarding.
  • Never roll more than 2 Chance or 2 Risk Dice. Multiple tags of the same kind don’t stack further; opposing tags cancel out.

Twist Counter

The Twist Counter tracks rising tension in the narrative. At the beginning it is set to 0. Every time doubles are rolled, the answer is “Yes, but…” and you add 1 to the Counter. When the Counter reaches 3, the answer is still “Yes, but…”, a Twist happens as well, and the Counter resets to 0.

Roll 2d6 and consult the following Twist Table to determine what kind of twist happens.

D6 Subject Action
1 A third party Appears
2 The Protagonist Alters the location
3 An encounter Helps the Protagonist
4 A physical event Hinders the Protagonist
5 An emotional event Changes the goal
6 An object Ends the scene

Interpret the two-word sentence in the context of the current scene. Before rolling, read the fiction: if the scene has been building pressure, the Twist lands hard and disruptively. If things have been relatively clean, it’s a shift, not a collapse.

Dead Ends

When a lead goes cold or a line of inquiry stops producing anything to act on, do not keep asking questions about the closed path. Instead, ask the Oracle: “Does something or someone point toward a new way forward?”

  • Yes (any) → Introduce a new element pointing forward.
  • No, but… → Path closed, but the Protagonist understands why.
  • No / No, and… → Pull back to the overall goal; start a new scene from a different angle.

Improvised Gadgets

The genre runs on heroes who build the way out from whatever is lying around. When your hero needs to improvise:

  1. Say what the gadget must do and what it is made of. If it is uncertain whether the materials are here, ask one framing question first: “Is there a … in this room?”
  2. Ask the Oracle whether the improvisation works. A relevant Skill (Jury-Rigging Genius, Kitchen Chemist) grants Advantage; working under fire or with the wrong parts imposes Disadvantage.
  3. Write the gadget as a tag (Microwave EMP, Fire-Extinguisher Slick). It is Gear for this scene only, and it grants Advantage when it bears on a roll.

Read the answer into the gadget itself. On Yes, but… it works once and then dies, or works and leaves something behind. On No, but… the parts are spent and the hero has learned what would work. A gadget that should outlive the scene becomes a real Gear trait at the end of the episode (see When the story ends).

Examples: a microwave turned into a short-range EMP, compressed air and PVC pipe made into a pneumatic launcher, a car stereo retuned as a radio jammer. Roll on Gadget Inspiration when you need a spark.

Vehicle Chases

Vehicle chases are Conflicts, resolved with any of the methods under Conflicts below. A vehicle is a character too, so give the important ones a sheet of their own.

  • With Harm & Luck, the Luck in play belongs to the drivers, not the vehicles. The vehicle supplies tags; the person behind the wheel takes the damage.
  • The vehicle’s tags (Turbo-Charged, Bulletproof Glass, Handles Like a Boat) grant Advantage or Disadvantage when they bear on the maneuver.
  • The road gets a vote: traffic, weather, and terrain are tags of the scene, and can impose Disadvantage when they stand between the hero and the getaway.
  • Spectacular crashes and narrow escapes are encouraged.
  • A vehicle that loses a chase can come out of it with a damage tag that persists until it’s repaired (see Status Track, below).

The Episode Structure

An episode can follow the classic TV structure. It isn’t a script: the Oracle still decides what happens. It tells you what kind of scene to frame next when you’re not sure.

  1. Cold Open: One short scene that sets the tone and the hook, often mid-action (see Cold Open Scenarios).
  2. Title Sequence: Not a scene. Name the episode (see Episode Titles) and take a moment to appreciate your hero’s style.
  3. Act I: The problem and the key NPCs come into view. Act I ends when the hero knows what the case is: open a Case Track.
  4. Act II: Investigation, complications, and the first real setback. Act II ends when the Case Track has one empty box left. Close that scene on a cliffhanger (see Cliffhanger Moments); the first scene of Act III answers it.
  5. Act III: The plan comes together and the final confrontation (see Climax Settings). It ends when the Case Track fills, or when the villain’s own countdown track fills first.
  6. Epilogue: A brief denouement showing the aftermath (see Epilogue Outcomes), then turn to When the story ends.

The acts read off the Case Track, so the structure needs no new bookkeeping. Acts only move forward: an erased box makes the case harder, not the episode shorter. If a Yes, and… jumps the track from two boxes to full, the case cracks wide open: play the confrontation as one scene and narrate it from that result, without a further roll. Scene Transition rolls still apply between scenes. A Quiet Scene in Act II is where the hero regroups; a Meanwhile in Act I is the villain’s establishing shot.

Conflicts

A Conflict is any situation in which opponents clash, attacking, defending, or wearing each other down in order to win, not only combat, but also competitive situations such as contests, duels, verbal confrontations, and vehicle chases.

Conflicts can be resolved in different ways:

  1. Ask a single closed question. The Oracle’s answer determines the outcome of the conflict.
  2. Ask a series of closed questions to resolve current single actions.
  3. Use the rules of Harm & Luck below.

Note that the Twist Counter does not apply to Harm & Luck. It is used regularly if the Conflict is handled with closed questions.

If the conflict is resolved by applying damage to the Luck trait, roll the dice to determine whether the protagonist causes damage to the opponent or suffers damage due to counterattack or failed defense. The rolls are player facing only.

The damage reduces the Luck of the target. A conflict ends when one side reaches 0 Luck, withdraws, or the opposition dissolves. Withdrawing is always allowed, and it is never free: breaking off before 0 Luck costs something; the fiction sets the price. When Luck runs out, the character has lost the conflict. The final outcome depends on the context: capture, injury, retreat, whatever fits the narrative.

Oracle Result Luck Damage
Yes, and… Deal 3 damage to opponent
Yes… Deal 2 damage to opponent
Yes, but… Deal 1 damage to opponent
No, but… Take 1 damage from opponent
No… Take 2 damage from opponent
No, and… Take 3 damage from opponent

Status Track (optional)

The Status Track gives lasting consequences a structured form. It does not replace Luck: Luck operates within a single conflict and resets at the end, while the Status Track records consequences that persist between conflicts, scene after scene.

When a defeat or setback should accumulate over time rather than resolve immediately, open a Status Track: a row of three boxes of escalating narrative pressure. Each time the Protagonist’s Luck reaches 0 in a conflict and the defeat leaves a lasting mark, fill the next empty box and assign the matching tag. One box per defeat, not per hit taken.

Box Physical Social Psychological
1 Hurt Rattled Unsettled
2 Injured On the Back Foot Shaken
3 Overcome Humiliated Broken

These are examples, not a fixed list. The active tag is always the most recently filled box: Hurt becomes Injured when the second box fills, not both at once, and it can impose Disadvantage when relevant. When the third box fills, the character is overcome; the story decides what that means.

Recovery: a consequence tag clears when the fiction supports it: rest, treatment, a quiet scene, or a scene framed around recovery. Clearing the current tag frees that box and restores the previous tag as active. Recovery needs a narrative justification, not just the passing of time.

Vehicles: an important vehicle keeps its own Physical track, reskinned as Dented, Smoking, Totaled. Repairs, not rest, clear it.

Case Tracks

Some cases are too complex to resolve in a single scene. When building toward a breakthrough will take sustained effort across multiple Acts or episodes, open a Case Track: a row of four empty boxes.

Once per scene, if advancing the case was the Protagonist’s stated purpose when the scene was framed, update the track based on the oracle result that resolved the scene’s main action.

Oracle Result Progress
Yes, and… Mark 2 boxes
Yes / Yes, but… Mark 1 box
No, but… No change
No / No, and… Erase 1 box

When all four boxes are filled, the case resolves. No final roll. A track can also run in reverse to represent a mounting threat or a villain’s plan coming to fruition.

Leverage (optional)

When the Oracle answers Yes, and…, you may bank that result as Leverage instead of narrating the bonus immediately. Hold only one Leverage at a time. It expires when its fictional basis no longer applies or when a Twist occurs.

Spend Leverage to:

  • Add Advantage to a future Oracle roll on the same pursuit (only if you don’t already have Advantage).
  • Convert a No or No, and… into No, but….

Scene Transition

At the end of the current scene, roll 1d6 and consult the following table:

D6 Next Scene
1-3 Dramatic scene
4-5 Quiet Scene
6 Meanwhile…
  • A dramatic scene does not break the tension of the previous scene but carries it further forward, introducing further obstacles or difficulties.
  • During a quiet scene there is time to take a breath, to heal, to make plans for the next steps and to deepen relationships.
  • A meanwhile scene is procedural, not atmospheric. Follow both steps: (1) Cut to whoever holds power over the situation (the villain, the client, the agency) and ask what they do next; update their tags to reflect their action. (2) Ask the Oracle: “Does an ally or wildcard act independently?” On a Yes of any kind, pick the NPC most affected by recent events and interpret their action through their current tags and goal. On a No, they hold.

Open-Ended Question or Get Inspired

To answer an Open-Ended question, roll once on each of the Inspiration Tables, 2d6 for row and column (roll at least a verb and a noun, adjectives are optional). Read the result back into the existing fiction as a lens, not an instruction to invent something new from scratch.

When the story ends

At the end of the adventure you may add another trait to the character related to how the story just ended: a new Skill, Gear, Frailty, or even a new Nemesis. You can also modify an existing trait to better represent an enhanced expertise.

Then update The Living World:

  • For each NPC or faction who played a meaningful role: update or assign a relationship tag; note what they want now; note whether they are gone, transformed, or still in play.
  • For each Location that featured in the episode: update its tags to reflect what happened there; note whether it remains accessible or relevant.
  • For each unresolved Event: note what pressure it continues to exert on the world.

The world doesn’t reset between sessions. Neither should your records of it.

Advanced Play Options

Series Campaign Rules

If you want to play Prime Time Hero as an ongoing series rather than one-shots, consider these additions:

Recurring Characters: Keep a stable of allies, contacts, and enemies who can appear across multiple episodes. Your helicopter pilot, your government contact, your reliable informant - they become part of your show’s “cast.”

Story Arcs: Plant seeds in early episodes that bloom into major storylines later. Maybe that corrupt businessman you exposed has a brother seeking revenge, or that experimental technology you destroyed was just a prototype.

Character Development: Growth happens at the end of each episode, as described in When the story ends: one new trait, or one existing trait rewritten. Across a season those changes add up. Good series-level changes include a new Skill learned on a case, Gear upgraded after a close call, a contact from a past episode earning a relationship tag, a new Nemesis made by the hero’s own actions, or a Frailty rewritten once an episode was really about facing it.

Reputation: As your hero becomes famous (or infamous), that fame can become a tag earned in play: Local Legend, Front-Page Face, Known to Interpol. Like any tag, it only matters when it’s relevant: Advantage when someone respects or fears the name, Disadvantage when the hero needs to go unnoticed, and a plot driver when enemies target the hero specifically.

Supporting Cast

Prime Time Hero is played with one Protagonist, but plenty of 1980s shows were team shows. You can capture that without changing the rules:

Team Format: The Protagonist leads a crew of specialists (the face, the tech expert, the driver, the muscle) in the mold of The A-Team. Teammates are NPCs with their own character sheets; when a teammate’s tags fit the job at hand, they can grant Advantage on the Protagonist’s roll.

Rival Format: A rival investigator works the same case from a different angle. Give them a sheet and cut to them in Meanwhile scenes; sometimes they’re a step ahead.

Guest Star Format: A recurring ally who has earned a character sheet can headline an episode of their own, with the regular Protagonist appearing as an NPC.

Difficulty Variants

Pulp Hero Mode: The hero survives what nobody should. Give them a standing tag, Pulp Hero, that grants Advantage when a stunt or a fight calls on larger-than-life nerve, the same way any other tag works, not a blanket bonus on every roll. When the hero’s Luck reaches 0, the defeat is a capture, a setback, or an escape at a cost, never a death.

Gritty Mode: The world is darker and more dangerous. Resolve every serious conflict with Harm & Luck, and use the Status Track as a core rule. Consequence tags clear only in a scene framed around recovery; a Quiet Scene alone isn’t enough.

Gadget Show Mode: For a series built around a talking car, a black helicopter, or a lab on wheels. One piece of the hero’s Gear is a character with a full sheet, its own Skills and Frailty, and a Goal that may not match the hero’s. Its tags work like any others, and so does its Frailty: the more the episode leans on the machine, the more often the machine is the problem.

Random Episode Generators

To open an episode at random, roll on Cold Open Scenarios; to name it, roll on Episode Titles. Both are D66 tables in Adventure Tables. For the case itself, roll on Case Generator.

Setting Variations

Tropical Paradise: Focus on island locations, resorts, and maritime adventures. Think Magnum P.I. with beach settings, yacht clubs, and helicopter tours.

Urban Jungle: City-based adventures with corporate espionage, high-tech crime, and metropolitan sophistication. Think Miami Vice meets Knight Rider.

International Intrigue: Globe-trotting adventures with diplomatic immunity, embassy parties, and exotic locations. Think James Bond on a network television budget.

Small Town Secrets: Rural or small-town setting where everyone knows everyone, and somebody always knows a little too much. Think Murder, She Wrote with a car chase in the second act.

Exploring the World

Every series has a home base, and every few episodes the network pays for a location shoot. A case that starts in a Honolulu bar can end in a Monaco penthouse or a jungle temple in Thailand. Travel is a scene like any other: frame it only when the trip itself can go wrong.

Preparation

  • Define Your Destination: Choose a specific location based on your current case, or roll on the Location tables for inspiration. Consider how this location ties into your investigation or mission.
  • Choose Your Mode of Travel: Your character likely has access to various vehicles - sports cars, helicopters, speedboats, or even private jets. Consider your resources and the urgency of the situation.
  • Make Contacts: Every location has local contacts, informants, and allies. Consider who might help you in this new place.

The Journey

A. Luxury Travel

  • Arriving in Style: First-class flights and five-star hotels are where the hero meets people. Ask the Oracle whether the trip turned up a useful acquaintance; on a Yes, add them as an NPC.
  • The Expense Account: When the bill matters, ask the Oracle whether the budget covers it. On a No, the hero has to cut corners, call in a favor, or find somewhere less comfortable to stay.

B. Covert Travel

  • Cover Identity: When traveling under a false name, ask the Oracle whether the cover holds. Relevant Skills and Gear (a Master of Disguise, a set of International Passports) grant Advantage.
  • Border Crossing: Customs, border guards, and hostile governments are obstacles like any other: frame a scene and use the tags that apply.

C. Emergency Travel

  • Finding a Ride: When the hero needs to get somewhere fast, ask the Oracle whether suitable transport is available. A Network of Contacts, or the right vehicle in their Gear, grants Advantage.
  • Pursuit: If someone tries to follow or stop the hero, it becomes a Vehicle Chase.

Reaching Your Destination

Upon reaching your destination, the location should feel like it jumped out of a 1980s travel brochure or spy thriller. Roll on location tables for specific details, or use the Oracle to determine what kind of scene awaits you.

1980s Locations

Four kinds of place cover most episodes. Pick one as the series’ home base and treat the others as guest locations.

Tropical Paradises

Locations like Hawaii, the Caribbean, or the French Riviera offer beach resorts, yacht clubs, and coral reefs hiding underwater secrets. Expect corrupt resort developers, international arms dealers, and mysterious archaeological sites.

International Cities

From London’s fog-shrouded streets to Tokyo’s neon-lit districts, major cities provide corporate headquarters, embassy parties, and underground criminal organizations. Fashion shows, art auctions, and diplomatic receptions serve as backdrops for espionage.

Remote Wilderness

Mountain lodges, desert research stations, and jungle expeditions offer isolation and danger. These locations often hide secret facilities, crashed aircraft, or ancient treasures that various parties want to control.

American Hotspots

Miami’s art deco hotels, Los Angeles’ entertainment industry, and New York’s financial district provide domestic adventure opportunities with international scope.

Quick Start Guide

Creating Your Prime Time Hero

  1. Choose or roll for a Name using the name tables
  2. Select a Concept that captures your character’s essence (like “Rogue CIA Agent” or “Hawaiian Private Eye”)
  3. Pick two Skills that make you special (like “Jury-Rigging Genius” or “Master of Disguise”)
  4. Choose one Frailty that creates interesting complications
  5. Select two pieces of Gear that define your style (like “Talking Sports Car” or “Swiss Army Knife”)
  6. Define your Goal (what you’re trying to achieve long-term)
  7. Establish your Motive (why you pursue this goal)
  8. Create or leave blank your Nemesis (they can emerge during play)
  9. Set Luck to 6

Your First Case

  1. Roll on the Case Generator or create your own hook
  2. Play the Cold Open: frame one short scene from the Cold Open Scenarios table
  3. Name the episode with the Episode Titles table
  4. Act I: find out who needs help and why, then open a Case Track
  5. Act II: work the case one scene at a time; ask the Oracle whenever the outcome is uncertain
  6. Act III: when the track has one box left, end the scene on a cliffhanger and go for the confrontation
  7. Epilogue: wrap up, add or rewrite one trait, and update the Living World

Essential Rules Summary

  • Oracle Rolls: 1 Chance Die vs 1 Risk Die; Chance higher is Yes, Risk higher is No; both low adds but…, both high adds and…
  • Advantage/Disadvantage: Roll a second Chance or Risk Die and keep the highest; never more than two of a kind; opposing tags cancel out
  • Twists: Equal dice read as Yes, but… and add 1 to the Twist Counter; at 3 a Twist occurs and the Counter resets
  • Conflicts: A single question, a series of questions, or Harm & Luck; Luck resets to 6 after every conflict
  • Case Tracks: Four boxes for cases too big for one scene; the acts of an episode follow the track
  • Improvised Gadgets: Describe your invention, ask the Oracle if it works, write it as a one-scene Gear tag
