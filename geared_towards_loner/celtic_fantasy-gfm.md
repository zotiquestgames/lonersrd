# Loner: Celtic Fantasy

## INTRODUCTION

***Loner: Celtic Fantasy*** is a solo tabletop RPG adventure set in the
Mythic Iron Age, a time when the Otherworld bleeds into mortal lands,
gods walk disguised as beggars, and a hero’s fate is written in the
strands of geasa they cannot refuse.

This minimalist Solo Role Playing Game is designed to tell **intimate,
tragic, heroic sagas** played by a single hand and guided by the voice
of the Morrigan herself (the Oracle).

*Loner: Celtic Fantasy* follows the following design principles:

1.  **Portable**: to play you will need a few common (six-sided) dice
    and writing materials. Anything else is optional and not essential.
2.  **Rules-Light**: the game relies on a few rules and only one solving
    mechanic, easy to learn and eventually to memorize.
3.  **Tag-based**: characters and situations are defined only by
    qualitative descriptors and no quantitative characteristics.
4.  **Emergent & Open-Ended**: there are no pre-written stories; every
    session generates its own narrative through choices and oracle
    answers.

With a focus on quick resolutions, **Loner: Celtic Fantasy** throws you
headfirst into the heart of legend. Your character will be defined by
thematic tags drawn from Celtic myth, like *“Truth-Bound Fili”*,
*“Landless Fianna”*, or *“Cannot Refuse Hospitality”*.

### What is a Role Playing Game (RPG)?

In a role-playing game, you play a character. The choices you make
determine what happens to them and to the world around them. You are not
reading a story someone else wrote; you are building it as you play.
Your character has a goal, abilities, and a world to move through. What
happens next is not predetermined.

Traditional role-playing games are played with a group and a Game Master
who prepares the world and referees what happens. *Loner: Celtic
Fantasy* removes both requirements. You play alone. The Oracle, a simple
dice mechanic, replaces the Game Master’s unpredictability. You are
player and storyteller both.



### What is a Solo RPG?

In a solo RPG a single player takes on the role of one or more
characters while also managing some elements of the game world. Unlike a
gamebook (such as the Fighting Fantasy, Lone Wolf, and Tunnels & Trolls
series) a solo RPG is not a form of interactive, forked narrative in
which outcomes are pre-determined and limited by the author’s choices.

Through the interaction of player, oracle, tools, and prompts, the
character’s actions will build an **emergent narrative** within whose
boundaries anything can be attempted, without predetermined limits.

### Playing Safely

You control what happens. *Loner: Celtic Fantasy* lets you explore any
story, but you also set the limits. One rule covers it: **if a scene
makes you uncomfortable, change it.** Rewind, reframe, or stop entirely.
The game is a tool for telling stories you want to tell; nothing here
requires you to push through something that doesn’t feel right.

### Minimum Requirements

To play *Loner: Celtic Fantasy* you will need:

- **4 six-sided dice** (also known as d6s): two pairs of different
  colors
- **Paper and writing tools**: at least a sheet of scrap paper and a
  pencil, but index cards or sticky notes are a fine addition
- **Character sheet**: you may use the provided sheet at the back or a
  simple index card. Write your Origin and your Geis next to your
  Concept.

Optional but useful:

- **Notebook**: *Loner: Celtic Fantasy* is not a solo journaling game,
  you can easily play it in the “theater of mind”. But keeping track of
  characters, locations, and ongoing storylines helps keep things
  consistent across sessions.

## THE MYTHIC IRON AGE

The world of *Loner: Celtic Fantasy* is not our history; it is **mythic
history**, where the impossible is commonplace and prophecy shapes
destiny more than choice.

### The World

**The Five Provinces** stand divided, each ruled by a chieftain
descended from gods or cursed by them. The land itself is alive:
**sacred wells grant visions, stone circles bind oaths, and the síd
(fairy mounds) hold kingdoms of ageless fae**.

- **The Mortal Realm**: Hills, forests, coasts, and ring-forts where
  humans eke survival and glory
- **The Otherworld**: Accessible through mist-shrouded thresholds, where
  time moves like honey and bargains made cannot be unmade
- **Liminal Spaces**: Crossroads at dusk, shorelines where sea meets
  land, the space between waking and dream, where fae and mortal meet
  and clash
- **Sacred Sites**: Stone circles, sacred wells, druid groves, the Hill
  of Tara, places where the veil thins and magic flows like water

### The Divine & the Cursed

The **Tuatha Dé Danann**, the divine folk, retreated into the síd mounds
after mortals claimed the land. They do not rule openly, but their
influence lingers. Druid rituals invoke their aid. Fae bargains bind
their strange logic. Every hero carries echoes of their blood or their
curse.

**The Morrigan**, triple goddess of war and fate, is ever-present,
appearing as raven, hag, or beautiful warrior. She does not guide
heroes; she *witnesses* them. The Oracle’s voice is hers.



### Playable Themes

- **Doom & Glory**: Heroes advance despite prophecies of failure
- **Clan & Exile**: Bonds of kinship tested by impossible choices
- **Geis & Obligation**: Sacred prohibitions that define identity
- **Cattle Raids & Vengeance**: Tribal conflict with supernatural stakes
- **Love & Loss**: Tragedy woven into the fabric of legend
- **The Otherworld Encounter**: Bargains with fae that exact prices in
  blood and time
- **Prophecy & Ríastrad**: Transformation through rage, magic, or cursed
  knowledge

### Timeline

**Before**: The Tuatha Dé ruled openly. The gods walked in daylight.

**The First Battle of Mag Tuired**: The Tuatha Dé win the land from the
Fir Bolg. Their king Nuada loses his arm, and with it the right to rule.

**The Second Battle of Mag Tuired**: Lugh slays Balor of the Evil Eye.
The Fomorians are driven into the sea, where they still wait.

**The Coming of the Sons of Míl**: Mortal invaders land at Beltane. The
poet Amergin sings the land into accepting them, and the Tuatha Dé are
defeated at Tailtiu.

**The Retreat** (mythic prehistory): The land is divided: mortals take
what lies above, the divine folk what lies below. They withdrew into the
síd. They did not disappear; they became hidden, jealous, hungry.

**The Heroic Age** (now): Five centuries after the retreat. Mortals
build kingdoms. Druids keep old knowledge. Heroes are born under ill
stars and strange geasa. The boundary between worlds grows thin once per
season.

## CHARACTERS

In *Loner: Celtic Fantasy*, your **Protagonist is defined by origin,
obligation, and doom**.

### Everything is a Character

In *Loner: Celtic Fantasy* Non-Playing Characters (NPCs), Foes,
Organizations, Monsters, and even relevant objects like chariots are
characters too! A **faerie queen**, a **blood-cursed sword**, and a
**rival clan** all have Concepts, Skills, Frailties, and Goals.

Your Protagonist is described by some fixed traits:

- **Name**: The name should be iconic and consistent with the tone and
  setting of the story. The Surnames table gives the forms for a man; a
  woman takes *Nic* for *Mac* and *Ní* for *Ó*, softening the next
  letter: *Mac Suibhne* becomes *Nic Shuibhne*, *Ó Briain* becomes *Ní
  Bhriain*.
- **Concept**: A concise description of the character’s profession,
  background, and abilities. The best are adjective-noun pairings, like
  *“Truth-Bound Fili”* or *“Landless Fianna”*. In this game the noun
  usually comes from your **Origin**, one of six Celtic archetypes that
  also supplies your Skills, Frailty, and Gear tables.
- **Skills** (x2): Abilities not necessarily character-specific but not
  characteristics common to all. *“Brave”* is not a skill,
  *“Spear-Dancer”* is.
- **Frailty**: Something that could potentially get in the way of the
  character, either physically, mentally, or socially.
- **Gear** (x2): Particular equipment supplied to the character in
  coherence with the setting. Everyday items are taken for granted and
  do not fall under this trait.
- **Geis**: A sacred prohibition or obligation laid on the character. It
  is a tag like any other: when it bears on the situation, it can grant
  Advantage or Disadvantage (see **The Geis System**).
- **Goal**: The long-term objective.
- **Motive**: What drives the pursuit of the goal.
- **Nemesis**: A person or organization that hinders the protagonist.
  Optional at character creation; it can emerge directly from play, and
  may or may not be the direct antagonist of the story.
- **Luck**: The measure of a character’s ability to avoid ill fortune or
  an inauspicious outcome. It applies only in Conflicts and resets fully
  when each conflict begins and ends. Luck starts and caps at 6.

These **traits** are described by **tags**, descriptive words or phrases
that can identify anything in the game world. Even the **details** of
the environment in which the action moves and **conditions** (physical
or mental) of the characters are tags.

**They are qualitative representations. They are not quantitative
measures.**

When a tag is relevant to a situation it may grant **Advantage or
Disadvantage** on an oracle roll. A tag can be real and present without
bearing on the current situation: relevance is the test, not presence.

### Relationships

When a connection with an NPC has been tested, strained, or deepened
through play, that relationship earns a tag: *Foster-Kin*, *Blood-Feud*,
*Guest-Bound*. Relationship tags function like any other: when they bear
directly on the situation, they grant Advantage or Disadvantage. They
are earned in the fiction, not assigned at creation.

### When to Promote a Tag to a Character

If an element matters once, keep it as plain tags. If it keeps showing
up or pushes back against the Protagonist, give it a character sheet and
play it by the same rules.

## RULES

*Loner: Celtic Fantasy* is a minimalist Solo Role Playing Game designed
to be played with only one character (the Protagonist). You’ll guide
them through the story that will unravel during the game, asking closed
questions to an Oracle which will help you overturn your expectations.
In this game, the Oracle speaks with the Morrigan’s voice.

Every now and then you will be surprised with an unexpected twist!

### Framing a Scene

Before asking the Oracle anything, establish the scene’s conditions.
Answer three questions:

- **Where** is the Protagonist, and what does that place feel like right
  now?
- **Who** is present, or likely to become present?
- **What** is the Protagonist’s goal for this scene?

Use what the fiction has established. If something is genuinely
uncertain, ask the Oracle one framing question before the scene begins.
A framing question must be about a fictional condition (what is already
true about the situation), not about what will happen. Once all three
are answered, the scene is open.

### Closing a Scene

A scene ends when one of the following is true:

- The Protagonist’s immediate goal has been **resolved**: achieved or
  definitively failed.
- The goal has been **blocked**: circumstances have made it impossible
  to pursue further here.
- The Protagonist **abandons** the goal and commits to a different
  course of action.

When any of these conditions is met, stop asking Oracle questions. Note
the outcome, then roll the Scene Transition. If none feel clearly met,
ask: *“Has this scene run its course?”* A Yes of any kind closes it. A
No means there is still something here worth pursuing.



### Consulting the Oracle

When you need to test the narrative, you’ll ask a **closed question**
(answerable with Yes or No) to the Oracle. Roll **1 Chance Die** and **1
Risk Die** of different colors, then follow these two steps.

- **Step 1: Compare the Dice**
  - Chance Die higher → **Yes**
  - Risk Die higher → **No**
  - Both equal → **Yes, but…** (+1 Twist Counter). Stop here; Step 2
    does not apply to equal rolls.
- **Step 2: Check for Modifiers** *(only when the dice are not equal)*
  - Both dice ≤ 3 → add *but…*
  - Both dice ≥ 4 → add *and…*

| Result                   | Outcome                      |
|--------------------------|------------------------------|
| Chance \> Risk           | Yes                          |
| Chance \> Risk, both ≤ 3 | Yes, but…                    |
| Chance \> Risk, both ≥ 4 | Yes, and…                    |
| Risk \> Chance           | No                           |
| Risk \> Chance, both ≤ 3 | No, but…                     |
| Risk \> Chance, both ≥ 4 | No, and…                     |
| Both equal               | Yes, but… (+1 Twist Counter) |

| Answer    | Outcome                                                 |
|-----------|---------------------------------------------------------|
| Yes, and… | You get what you want, and something extra.             |
| Yes…      | You get what you want.                                  |
| Yes, but… | You get what you want, but at a cost.                   |
| No, but…  | You don’t get what you want, but it’s not a total loss. |
| No…       | You don’t get what you were after.                      |
| No, and…  | You don’t get what you want, and things get worse.      |



### Advantage and Disadvantage

Tags, conditions, and circumstances can grant **Advantage** or
**Disadvantage**.

- **Advantage** → Roll 2 Chance Dice, keep the highest.
- **Disadvantage** → Roll 2 Risk Dice, keep the highest.
- Read the result from the kept die only. A discarded die counts for
  nothing: equal dice and the *but…/and…* bands are checked after
  discarding.
- Never roll more than 2 Chance or 2 Risk Dice. Multiple tags of the
  same kind don’t stack further; opposing tags cancel out.

**Example**: Your *Cattle-Raid Veteran* fianna is sneaking into a rival
rath at night (*Advantage*: the night is *Moonless*, and they are a
*Cattle-Raid Veteran*; two tags of the same kind don’t stack) but the
chieftain has posted guards (*Disadvantage*: *Alert Sentries*).
Advantage and Disadvantage cancel out; roll one Chance Die and one Risk
Die as normal.

### Twist Counter: When Ríastrad Rises

The Twist Counter tracks rising tension in the narrative. At the
beginning it is set to 0. Every time doubles are rolled, add 1 to the
Counter. If the Counter is below 3, treat the result as **“Yes, but…”**.
When the Counter reaches 3, a **Twist** happens and the Counter resets
to 0.

Roll 2d6 and consult the following **Celtic Twist Table** to determine
what kind of twist happens.

| D6  |         Subject         |               Action               |
|:---:|:-----------------------:|:----------------------------------:|
|  1  |    One of the Sídhe     |      Appears Without Warning       |
|  2  |     The Protagonist     | Alters the Land (the veil shifts)  |
|  3  |      The Morrigan       |       Helps the Protagonist        |
|  4  |   An Ancient Prophecy   |      Hinders the Protagonist       |
|  5  | The Veil Between Worlds | Triggers a Geis (changes the goal) |
|  6  |      A Sacred Oath      | Proclaims a Doom (ends the scene)  |

Interpret the two-word sentence in the context of the current scene.
Before rolling, read the fiction: if the scene has been building
pressure, the Twist lands hard and disruptively. If things have been
relatively clean, it’s a shift, not a collapse.

### Example: Twist in Action

> **Scene**: Your fili is negotiating safe passage through a rival
> territory. The Twist Counter is already at 2.
>
> **Question**: *“Does the chieftain grant safe passage?”*
>
> **Roll**: (3, 3) → Doubles! **Yes, but…**, and the Counter reaches 3:
> a **Twist** happens and the Counter resets to 0.
>
> **Twist Rolls**: 2d6 = (5, 5) → Subject: **“The Veil Between
> Worlds”** + Action: **“Triggers a Geis”**
>
> **Interpretation**: The chieftain *does* grant safe passage, but as
> you speak the veil between worlds grows thin and your own geis flares.
> Some prohibition you carry is now in conflict with the terms you just
> agreed to, and your goal for this journey has to change.

### Dead Ends

When a lead goes cold or a line of inquiry stops producing anything to
act on, do not keep asking questions about the closed path. Instead, ask
the Oracle: *“Does something or someone point toward a new way
forward?”*

- **Yes** (any) → Introduce a new element pointing forward.
- **No, but…** → Path closed, but the Protagonist understands why.
- **No / No, and…** → Pull back to the overall goal; start a new scene
  from a different angle.

### The Geis System

A **geis** (pl. *geasa*) is a **sacred prohibition or obligation** that
defines your hero’s fate. It is the most Celtic element of character
creation.

#### Choosing Your Geis

Pick or roll one from this list (or create your own). For the geasa that
bind NPCs, roll on **Geasa of Others**.

| D6  |                        Geis                        |
|:---:|:--------------------------------------------------:|
|  1  |             Cannot Refuse Hospitality              |
|  2  | Cannot Refuse a Challenge (from any rival or bard) |
|  3  |          Must Protect Those of Your Blood          |
|  4  |          Cannot Break an Oath Once Sworn           |
|  5  |      Must Complete Any Quest Given by \[NPC\]      |
|  6  |       Cannot Lie (or Cannot Tell the Truth)        |

#### How Geis Works Mechanically

1.  **While you honor your geis**, it functions as a tag: when honoring
    the geis is what the roll is about (accepting the challenge,
    receiving the hospitality, keeping the oath), it grants
    **Advantage**, same as any Skill.
2.  **If you break your geis**, you gain a tag such as *Oath-Broken* or
    *Doom-Marked*. That tag grants **Disadvantage** only when it bears
    on the roll at hand, not on everything you do. The tag clears when
    you complete a quest of atonement or perform a ritual to restore
    yourself, never just by waiting. A broken geis is also the surest
    way to lose the conflicts that fill the **Doom** column of the
    Status Track: when a defeat comes because fate turned against you,
    mark it there.
3.  **Some geasa are double-edged**: *“You cannot refuse hospitality”*
    protects you (enemies must shelter you) but forces you into
    dangerous situations (you cannot decline an enemy’s invitation).

#### Example: The Champion’s Geis

> **Geis**: *“Cannot Refuse a Challenge”*
>
> **Honoring It**: When a rival insults you or a bard proclaims your
> cowardice, you **gain Advantage** on any roll to accept and face the
> challenge.
>
> **Breaking It**: You refuse a challenge to save your life. You gain
> the tag *Oath-Broken*, which grants Disadvantage whenever your honor
> or your word is what’s on the line, until you face that challenger
> (accepting near-certain death if necessary) or complete an atoning
> ritual.

### The Otherworld Overlay

The Otherworld is not a location you stumble into; it is a **state of
being**, reached through **threshold spaces** at specific times.

#### Entering the Otherworld

You can enter the Otherworld through:

- **Síd mounds** at dawn, dusk, or the turning seasons
- **Sacred wells** with proper ritual
- **Enchanted mists** that rise without warning
- **Dreams** (you physically enter while asleep)
- **Fae invitation** (impossible to refuse, always costly)

When you enter, **note it clearly**. The Otherworld operates by
different rules. To find out where the threshold leads, roll on
**Otherworld Realms**; for who meets you there, roll on **Otherworld
Denizens**.



#### Time Distortion

In the Otherworld, **time moves like honey**. A scene that feels like
hours might be minutes in the mortal world, or a night in the Otherworld
equals a year in the lands of men.

**Mechanically**: When you leave the Otherworld, ask the Oracle a closed
question such as *“Has a year or more passed in the mortal realm?”* The
answer shapes what you return to.

#### Reading the Oracle in Fae Bargains

The Oracle works unchanged when you deal with the fae; only the way you
read its answers shifts. The fae never lie, so every Yes is true to the
letter, and every result is read through their strange logic:

- **“Yes, but…”** is the fae’s natural answer: you get your wish, but at
  a price. The price is real and named now, even if it falls due later.
- **“Yes, and…”** gives you more than you asked for. With the fae, the
  extra is often their attention: a relationship tag such as *Noticed by
  the Court* can open doors as easily as it draws hunters.
- **“No, and…”** is where the fae turn a refusal into harm: they offer
  you *what you asked for, but not what you wanted*, and taking it makes
  things worse.

When the price is not obvious from the fiction, roll on **Fae Prices**.

#### Example: A Fae Bargain

> **You**: *“Will the crone give me the magic that saves my sister?”*
>
> **Oracle**: Yes, but…
>
> **Interpretation**: You receive the magic, and it saves your sister’s
> life, but the moment it works **you are bound to the crone’s service
> for seven years**. You didn’t ask about the cost; the fae didn’t offer
> it. That’s how bargains work. You gain the tag *Crone-Bound*.

#### Realm-Specific Tags

Certain **tags only function in the Otherworld** (or only in the mortal
realm):

- *Fae-Form* works only in the Otherworld
- *Iron-Forged* provides Advantage only against fae
- *Mortal-Binding* loses power in the Otherworld

When crossing realms, consider how your tags shift in meaning.



### Seasonal Influence: The Celtic Wheel

The **Celtic calendar** shapes what is possible in your story. Note the
current season as you play.

#### The Four Seasons

| Season | When | Significance |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| **Samhain** | Autumn/Winter Turn | The veil thins. Dead walk. Fae hunt. |
| **Imbolc** | Winter/Spring Turn | First stirring. Prophecy speaks. Druids divine the year ahead. |
| **Beltane** | Spring/Summer Turn | The great fire feast. Herds sacred. Love and war intertwine. |
| **Lughnasadh** | Summer/Autumn Turn | Harvest begins. Games and challenges. First death of summer (the god Lugh). |

The season colors the mood of every scene and the kind of encounters
likely to arise: use it to frame scenes and to interpret Oracle results.
A season does not grant blanket Advantage or Disadvantage on its own. If
a specific tag tied to the season, *Veil-Thin* at Samhain, *Divining* at
Imbolc, is relevant to a single roll, it grants Advantage or
Disadvantage exactly like any other tag, for that roll only, not for
every roll made until the season turns.

**Advancing Seasons**: Begin play in any season that fits your saga’s
opening. After a major arc concludes, a geis is broken or fulfilled, or
the Oracle signals that enough time has passed, advance to the next
season. The wheel turns: Samhain → Imbolc → Beltane → Lughnasadh →
Samhain.

When a festival falls in the current scene, roll on **Festivals &
Seasonal Events**: one die for the row (1 Samhain, 2 Imbolc, 3 Beltane,
4 Lughnasadh, 5 or 6 between the seasons), one for the event.



### Conflicts: Facing Doom With Glory

A *Conflict* is any situation in which opponents clash, attacking,
defending, or wearing each other down in order to win: not only combat,
but also competitive situations such as contests, duels, and verbal
confrontations.

Conflicts are **not limited to fighting**. A verbal confrontation with a
druid, a chase through Otherworldly mist, or a test of wills with a fae
lord are all Conflicts.

Conflicts can be resolved in different ways:

1.  Ask a single closed question. The Oracle’s answer determines the
    outcome of the conflict.
2.  Ask a series of closed questions to resolve current single actions.
3.  Use the rules of Harm & Luck below.

Note that the Twist Counter **does not apply** to Harm & Luck. It is
used regularly if the Conflict is handled with closed questions.

If the conflict is resolved by applying damage to the Luck trait, roll
the dice to determine whether the protagonist causes damage to the
opponent or suffers damage due to counterattack or failed defense. The
rolls are player facing only.

The damage reduces the Luck of the target. A conflict ends when one side
reaches 0 Luck, withdraws, or the opposition dissolves. Withdrawing is
always allowed, and it is never free: breaking off before 0 Luck costs
something; the fiction sets the price. When Luck runs out, the character
has lost the conflict. The final outcome depends on the context:
capture, injury, retreat, whatever fits the saga.

| **Oracle Result** | **Luck Damage**                 |
|-------------------|---------------------------------|
| **Yes, and…**     | Deal **3** damage to opponent   |
| **Yes…**          | Deal **2** damage to opponent   |
| **Yes, but…**     | Deal **1** damage to opponent   |
| **No, but…**      | Take **1** damage from opponent |
| **No…**           | Take **2** damage from opponent |
| **No, and…**      | Take **3** damage from opponent |

Luck resets to 6 after each Conflict ends, regardless of outcome.
Lasting fallout from a defeat does not linger as a drained number: it
becomes a tag, tracked on the Status Track below.

#### Example: Spear-Dance at the Ford

> **Your fianna faces a rival warrior in ritual combat at a ford.**
>
> **Round 1**: *“Do I land the first blow?”*\
> Roll: Chance (5) vs Risk (2) → **Yes**\
> → You deal **2 Luck damage**. Rival’s Luck: 4/6
>
> **Round 2**: *“Do I turn aside the rival’s counterattack?”*\
> Roll: Chance (1) vs Risk (3) → **No, but…**\
> → The blow grazes you: you take **1 Luck damage**. Your Luck: 5/6.
> **But** the rival’s spear-shaft cracks against your shield, adding a
> **tag**: *Weapon-Broken*.
>
> **Round 3**: *“Does my spear-dance overwhelm them?”* Your
> *Spear-Dancer* Skill grants Advantage: roll 2 Chance Dice and keep the
> highest.\
> Roll: Chance (6, 2 → keep 6) vs Risk (4) → **Yes, and…**\
> → Deal **3 Luck damage**, and the rival gains the tag *Terrified*.
> Rival’s Luck: 1/6
>
> **Round 4**: *“Do I force them to yield?”*\
> Roll: Chance (4) vs Risk (4) → **Yes, but…** (the Twist Counter does
> not apply to Harm & Luck)\
> → Deal **1 Luck damage**. Rival’s Luck: 0/6. They **yield to save
> their life**, but in doing so they **swear a blood oath of fealty to
> you**, and a geis now binds you both.
>
> The conflict is over. Your Luck resets to 6.

### Status Track

The Status Track gives lasting consequences a structured form. It does
not replace Luck: Luck operates within a single conflict and resets at
the end, while the Status Track records consequences that persist
between conflicts, scene after scene.

When a defeat or setback should accumulate over time rather than resolve
immediately, open a Status Track: a row of three boxes of escalating
narrative pressure. Each time the Protagonist’s Luck reaches 0 in a
conflict and the defeat leaves a lasting mark, fill the next empty box
and assign the matching tag. One box per defeat, not per hit taken.

| Box | Physical   | Honor       | Doom         |
|-----|------------|-------------|--------------|
| 1   | *Hurt*     | *Shamed*    | *Marked*     |
| 2   | *Injured*  | *Disgraced* | *Fate-Bound* |
| 3   | *Overcome* | *Outcast*   | *Doomed*     |

These are examples, not a fixed list. The active tag is always the most
recently filled box: *Shamed* becomes *Disgraced* when the second box
fills, not both at once, and it can impose Disadvantage when relevant.
When the third box fills, the character is overcome; the saga decides
what that means. Use the Doom column when a defeat comes from fate
turning against you, most often a conflict lost with a broken geis
weighing on you (see **The Geis System**).

**Recovery**: a consequence tag clears when the fiction supports it,
rest, an act of atonement, a quiet scene, or a scene framed around
recovery. Clearing the current tag frees that box and restores the
previous tag as active. Recovery needs a narrative justification, not
just the passing of time.

### Scene Transition

At the end of the current scene, roll 1d6 and consult the following
table:

| D6  |   Next Scene   |
|:---:|:--------------:|
| 1-3 | Dramatic scene |
| 4-5 |  Quiet Scene   |
|  6  |   Meanwhile…   |

- A **dramatic scene** does not break the tension of the previous scene
  but carries it further forward, introducing further obstacles or
  difficulties.
- During a **quiet scene** there is time to take a breath, to heal, to
  make plans for the next steps and to deepen relationships.
- A **meanwhile scene** is procedural, not atmospheric. Follow both
  steps: (1) Cut to whoever holds power over the situation (a rival
  chieftain, a fae lord, the Morrigan herself) and ask what they do
  next, then update their tags to reflect their action. (2) Ask the
  Oracle: *“Does an ally or wildcard act independently?”* On a Yes of
  any kind, pick the NPC most affected by recent events and interpret
  their action through their current tags and goal. On a No, they hold.

When whoever holds power is your Nemesis, roll on **Enemy Schemes** for
what they do next.

### Open-Ended Question or Get Inspired

To answer an Open-Ended question (e.g., *“What does the mist reveal?”*),
roll once on each of the Inspiration Tables, 2d6 for row and column
(roll at least a verb and a noun, adjectives are optional). Read the
result back into the existing fiction as a lens, not an instruction to
invent something new from scratch.

*“Transforms + Hungry + Raven”* at a síd mound might mean the raven
perched on the lintel is no bird at all, and it has been waiting for
someone to feed it.

Verbs & Actions, Adjectives & Qualities, and Nouns & Concepts are the
core three Inspiration Tables, at the end of the Adventure Tables; the
other seven are there when a question calls for them.

### When the Story Ends: Saga Advancement

At the end of the adventure, whether in triumph, tragedy, or ambiguous
closure, you may add another trait to the character related to how the
story just ended: a new Skill, Gear, Frailty, or even a new Nemesis. In
this game, a new **Geis** is also a fitting mark of a saga’s end. You
can also modify an existing trait to better represent an enhanced
expertise.

Then update **The Living World**:

- For each NPC or faction who played a meaningful role: update or assign
  a relationship tag; note what they want now; note whether they are
  gone, transformed, or still in play.
- For each Location that featured in the story: update its tags to
  reflect what happened there; note whether it remains accessible or
  relevant.
- For each unresolved Event: note what pressure it continues to exert on
  the world. Did you shift the balance between clans, anger the fae,
  break a prophecy or fulfill it?

The world doesn’t reset between sessions. Neither should your records of
it.

## FACTIONS & ORGANIZATIONS

Every faction is a character, built with the same traits as your
Protagonist. When one pushes back against you, play it by the same
rules.

### The Druid Order

- **Concept**: Venerable Law-Keepers
- **Skills**: Law-Speaking, Sacred Rites
- **Frailty**: Bound by Neutrality
- **Gear**: Oak Groves, Ogham Archives
- **Goal**: Keep the balance between the mortal world and the Otherworld
- **Motive**: Without the old law, the veil tears
- **Nemesis**: Corrupted druids who sell their rites

The druids answer to no king, and every king answers to them. They judge
disputes, name the days for war and for peace, and speak the rites that
keep the síd doors shut. A druid can halt a battle by walking between
the armies. Their council meets in secret, and whatever it decides
reaches the provinces as omens nobody may question.

### The Filid

- **Concept**: Feared Poet-Guild
- **Skills**: Satire That Wounds, Memory of Genealogies
- **Frailty**: Must Be Paid in Honor
- **Gear**: Branch of Silver Bells, Feathered Poet’s Cloak
- **Goal**: Hold the right to praise and to shame every ruler
- **Motive**: Whoever tells the story owns the land
- **Nemesis**: Chieftains who refuse their due

A poet of the highest rank, an ollamh, travels with a retinue and
expects to be fed like a king. Refuse and the satire comes: verses that
raise blisters on a face and strip a king of his right to rule. The
filid remember every lineage, and they can erase one.

### The Fianna

- **Concept**: Wild Warrior-Bands
- **Skills**: Forest Warfare, Hound-Mastery
- **Frailty**: Landless and Lawless
- **Gear**: Hunting Hounds, Hidden Camps
- **Goal**: Guard the provinces from raiders and monsters, on their own
  terms
- **Motive**: Honor, and the freedom of the woods
- **Nemesis**: Chieftains who would tame them

From Beltane to Samhain the Fianna live in the wild, hunting and
fighting for whoever feeds them in winter. Joining means passing tests
that kill the unworthy. They are protectors in one valley and bandits in
the next, and nobody in the band agrees who leads them since the last
captain fell.

### The Court of Tara

- **Concept**: Ambitious High Kingship
- **Skills**: Summoning Hosts, Rites of Sovereignty
- **Frailty**: The King Must Stay Unblemished
- **Gear**: The Stone of Fál, Hostages From Every Province
- **Goal**: Bind all Five Provinces under Tara
- **Motive**: The land thrives only under a true king
- **Nemesis**: Provincial kings who refuse to bend

The High King sits on the hill at the center of the world, fed by
tribute from all five provinces. A scar, a lost hand, a broken geis: any
blemish ends a reign, so the court is a nest of hidden flaws and
whispering heirs. The Stone of Fál cries out under the true king. Lately
it has been silent.

### The Courts of the Síd

- **Concept**: Ageless Fae Nobility
- **Skills**: Glamour, Binding Bargains
- **Frailty**: Bound by Their Own Word
- **Gear**: Silver Branches, The Host of the Air
- **Goal**: Take back what mortals stole
- **Motive**: The land was theirs first
- **Nemesis**: The Druid Order

Beneath the hills, the Tuatha Dé and their kin hold court in halls where
no one ages. They are not one people but many rival houses, each with
its own grudges against the others and against the mortals above. They
rarely strike openly. They invite, bargain, and wait, and their patience
is longer than any mortal line.

### The Children of Balor

- **Concept**: Drowned Old Enemy
- **Skills**: Sea-Storms, Blight and Famine
- **Frailty**: Weakened by Sunlight
- **Gear**: Sunken Halls, Cursed Tribute
- **Goal**: Rise from the sea and reclaim the land
- **Motive**: Vengeance for Mag Tuired
- **Nemesis**: The heirs of Lugh

The Fomorians lost the land at the Second Battle of Mag Tuired, and
their survivors went down into the sea. Coastal villages still leave
them offerings to keep the storms away. Some chieftains, desperate or
ambitious, have begun to pay them more.

## POLITICAL ENTITIES

The Five Provinces are each ruled by their own king, and all five owe
tribute to Tara, when they can be made to pay it.

- **Ulaid (Ulster)**: The north. The warriors of the Red Branch feast at
  Emain Macha, proud and quick to fight. Old enemies of Connacht.
- **Connacht**: The west. A queen rules from Cruachan, where a cave
  leads down into the Otherworld. She counts her wealth in cattle and
  wants more.
- **Laigin (Leinster)**: The east. Rich plains, a long coast, and a
  bitter grudge over the cattle-tribute Tara takes each year.
- **Mumu (Munster)**: The south. Druids and old magic; more síd mounds
  here than anywhere else, and the fae are closer.
- **Mide (Meath)**: The center, the High King’s own land around Tara,
  and the fire of Uisnech that relights every hearth.

## APPENDIX: GLOSSARY OF CELTIC TERMS

**Bean Nighe**: A spirit who appears as a small woman washing bloodied
clothes at a ford or stream; seeing her foretells the death of someone
in your bloodline.

**Beltane**: Spring festival (May 1st) marking the beginning of summer,
associated with fertility, cattle blessing, and the thinning of the veil
between worlds.

**Brehon**: A judge and expert in the old law; a brehon’s ruling binds
even a king.

**Caoránach**: A plague-beast from Irish myth, associated with
pestilence and the dark waters of Lough Derg, also called the Mother of
Demons; she spreads disease and corruption wherever she walks.

**Crannóg**: An artificial island built in lakes or wetlands, typically
fortified and used as a dwelling or defensive stronghold.

**Dagda**: The “good god” of the Tuatha Dé, father of many of them,
owner of a cauldron that is never empty and a club that kills with one
end and revives with the other.

**Daoine Sídhe**: The people of the síd: supernatural beings of great
power and beauty who follow their own mysterious laws.

**Dearg-Due**: A blood-drinking revenant of Irish folklore; the stones
piled on her grave keep her from rising.

**Drui** (pl. **drui**): Druids, the learned class of Celtic society who
served as priests, judges, advisors, and keepers of sacred knowledge.

**Dullahan**: A headless horseman of Irish folklore who rides a black
horse and carries his own severed head, serving as a harbinger of death.

**Fachan**: A creature of Gaelic folklore with one eye, one hand, and
one leg, who strikes travelers from the dusk.

**Fetch**: The spectral double of a living person; meeting it foretells
that person’s death.

**Fianna**: Bands of landless warriors living outside clan structure,
often young men exiled or seeking glory before inheriting land; famous
for their martial prowess and wild living.

**Fidchell**: A board game of strategy played by kings and heroes, often
for high stakes.

**Fili** (pl. **Filid**): Poet-sorcerers who wielded power through
words, verse, and satire; members of the learned class alongside druids
with mastery over memory, prophecy, and enchantment.

**Fomorians**: Ancient chaotic beings, often depicted as monstrous or
demonic, who represented the destructive forces of nature and were
enemies of the Tuatha Dé Danann.

**Geis** (pl. **Geasa**): A sacred prohibition or obligation placed upon
an individual, often from birth; breaking a geis brings doom, while
honoring it grants power and fate’s favor.

**Imbas Forosnai**: A poet’s rite of illumination: chewing raw flesh,
then sleeping to receive a vision.

**Imbolc**: Winter festival (February 1st) celebrating the first
stirrings of spring, associated with the goddess Brigid, purification,
and prophetic divination.

**Leanan Sídhe**: A supernatural muse from the Irish Otherworld who
inspires mortal artists to greatness while consuming their life force;
her lovers create brilliantly but die young.

**Lughnasadh**: Harvest festival (August 1st) celebrating the grain
harvest with athletic games, feasting, and contests in honor of the god
Lugh.

**Manannán**: Manannán mac Lir, god of the sea and lord of the
Otherworld isles, who rides the waves as if they were a plain.

**Merrow**: A sea-maiden of Irish folklore; her magic cap lets her live
beneath the waves, and whoever takes it keeps her on land.

**Morrigan** (also **Morrígu**): The triple goddess of war, fate, and
sovereignty who appears as crow, raven, or woman; she does not aid
heroes but witnesses their doom and glory.

**Óenach**: A great seasonal assembly for law, trade, games, and horse
races.

**Ogham**: Ancient Irish alphabet carved into standing stones and wooden
staffs, used for inscriptions, magical purposes, and divination.

**Ollamh**: The highest rank of poet, or of any master of a learned art.

**Péist**: A monstrous serpent or water-beast of Irish lore, said to
lurk in lakes.

**Púca** (also **Pooka**): A shape-shifting trickster spirit of Irish
folklore, often appearing as a horse, goat, or goblin; mischievous and
dangerous to those who offend it.

**Ríastrad**: The “warp-spasm” or battle-fury, a supernatural
transformation that overcomes certain warriors in combat, granting
terrible strength but at great cost to body and mind.

**Samhain**: Autumn festival (October 31st/November 1st) marking the end
of harvest and beginning of winter; the veil between worlds is thinnest,
and the dead walk among the living.

**Selkie**: Shape-changers who appear as seals in water but can shed
their skin to take human form on land; if their seal-skin is stolen,
they cannot return to the sea.

**Siabhróg**: Malevolent sprites or will-o’-wisps from Irish folklore,
often travelling in swarms; they lure travelers astray with confusion
and panic, feeding on the chaos they create.

**Síd**: A fairy mound or hollow hill, where the Tuatha Dé Danann
retreated after mortals claimed the land above; the Síd also means the
realm and courts beneath the hills.

**Sídhe** (pronounced “shee”): The folk of the síd, the fairy people;
also used in names such as Daoine Sídhe and Leanan Sídhe.

**Tanist**: The heir chosen in advance to succeed a chieftain or king.

**Tír na nÓg**: The “Land of Youth,” an Otherworldly realm where time
moves differently and inhabitants never age; accessible only through
magical means or invitation.

**Torc**: A rigid neck-ring of precious metal worn by Celtic nobility as
a symbol of status, authority, and often magical power.

**Tuatha Dé Danann**: The “People of the Goddess Danu,” divine or
semi-divine beings who ruled Ireland before retreating into the síd
mounds; they possess great magical powers and long lives.
